Before Your First Dial

Chargebacks, explained before you need to know

Every recruiting ad talks about commission. Almost none mention this. Here it is first, so your first cancelled policy is an annoyance instead of a crisis.

What an advance actually is

When a policy issues, you're usually paid several months of premium up front rather than as the client pays. That's an advance — money fronted against premium nobody has paid yet.

It feels like a paycheck. It isn't one.

What a chargeback is

If the client stops paying before the advance period is up, the unearned part comes back — out of your next commissions, or out of your pocket if there aren't any.

That's a chargeback. Not a scam, not unique to any company, and normal in every commission business that advances money. The only unusual thing about our industry is how rarely anyone mentions it before you start.

If a policy lapses in month three and you were advanced nine, you owe back roughly six months of that commission.

The math on one policy

Annual premium$1,200
Your commission rate100%
Advance (9 of 12 months)$900
Client pays 3 months, then lapses$300 earned
Chargeback owed$600

Illustration only — advance percentages, commission rates and chargeback periods vary by carrier and by product. Ask for the specifics on anything you're about to write.

Why policies lapse

  • The draft date doesn't match payday. The most common and most preventable cause.
  • The client isn't connected to their agent. When something changes, they need to be able to reach you in ten seconds. If you aren't saved in their phone, you aren't who they call.
  • They didn't fully understand what they bought. A sale that needed talking into gets talked out of.
  • Money got tight. Sometimes there's nothing you could have done.
  • Nobody followed up. A first-payment check-in prevents more lapses than any rebuttal.

How to not get hurt by this

  • Don't spend an advance like it's earned. Set aside a share of every advance until the chargeback period passes. Agents who do this ride out a bad month; agents who don't go into debt over one.
  • When possible, set the draft date to coincide with payday. Especially on Social Security or disability — have it come out the same day the money lands. Nothing else you do all week protects more business.
  • Make sure they can find you. Digital business card, your name and number saved in their phone before you leave. A client who can reach their agent calls before they cancel.
  • Call after the first payment clears. Two minutes. Confirm it went through, confirm they know what they bought.
  • Get them set up with Legacy Safeguard. Part of making sure a family is actually taken care of, not just sold to.
  • Sell people who want it. A policy you pressured someone into is a chargeback with a delay on it.
  • Know your own numbers. Your advance percentage, your chargeback period, and what happens to a balance if you leave. Ask before you sign, not after.

Persistency is the real job

Chargebacks are a symptom. The thing underneath them is persistency — whether the business you wrote is still on the books a year from now.

That comes down to whether your clients feel serviced. They should know who you are, know how to reach you, and be sure you're there if something happens. An agent who services well has fewer lapses, fewer chargebacks and a book that compounds. An agent who writes and disappears rebuilds their income from zero every month.

The honest version

You can have a great month, spend it, and get a bill the following quarter for business that fell off. That happens to people. It's the single most common way new agents end up in debt in this business.

It's also completely manageable once you know it's coming — which is the entire reason this page exists.
